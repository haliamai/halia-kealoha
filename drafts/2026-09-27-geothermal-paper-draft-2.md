Should Hawaiʻi Keep Funding Geothermal Resource Characterization?
Hawaiʻi has already put $5 million into characterizing geothermal resources on Maui through slim-hole drilling, subsurface and water testing, data analysis, updated conceptual models, and recommendations (Hawaiʻi State Energy Office [HSEO], n.d.; Hawaiʻi Department of Business, Economic Development & Tourism [DBEDT], 2025). The real question isn't whether geothermal energy could someday help Hawaiʻi. It's whether spending public money to reduce our uncertainty about the resource is worth it, and whether the state should follow up with another round of comparable funding.
My recommendation: finish and evaluate the current $5 million program before committing to another one. If the results end up justifying more investment, any future characterization funding should come with two strings attached: the information it produces needs to be accessible and reusable, and there needs to be transparent reporting on who actually benefits from that value and how it's distributed.
What $5 Million Actually Buys
It's worth being precise about what this money is for. It's not building a power plant. It's paying to learn more about what's actually down there.
That distinction matters economically. Information itself can be valuable before any commercial project ever breaks ground. Geothermal development takes a lot of upfront exploration money, and a lot of that spending happens while big questions about the resource are still unanswered. Better information lets developers and policymakers make sharper calls with less guesswork, including the call to walk away if the resource doesn't pan out.
There's also a spillover benefit when that information isn't locked up with one company. The Department of Energy's Geothermal Data Repository gives the public access to data from DOE-funded geothermal projects (U.S. Department of Energy [DOE], n.d.). I read that as a public-good argument in action: information gets more valuable once it's not just useful to whoever happened to fund it first. That said, this is my read of what DOE's program structure implies, not a claim that DOE frames it as fixing a market failure.
That distinction matters because I didn't find any evidence that private geothermal developers in Hawaiʻi are systematically underinvesting in characterization. High upfront costs and uncertainty aren't, by themselves, proof that the government needs to step in. So I'm not treating the existence of the current program as automatic justification for a second round.
What Would Have to Be True for a Second $5 Million to Pay Off?
The two numbers that matter most can't be pinned down with any real confidence right now. One is how much publicly funded characterization actually moves the needle on a project's odds of succeeding commercially — call that Δp. The other is the total public value created if the project does succeed — V_public.
Instead of forcing a single estimate out of numbers that don't support one, I ran a sensitivity analysis:
Expected public value from characterization = Δp × V_public
Net expected public value = (Δp × V_public) − $5 million
Which means the break-even point is:
Δp* = $5 million / V_public

I tested this against assumed public values of $10 million, $25 million, $50 million, and $100 million — not because those are real estimates of what a Maui project would generate, but to see how the answer shifts. As Figure 1 shows, at $10 million in public value, characterization would need to improve the odds of success by 50 percentage points to break even. At $25 million, that drops to 20 points. At $50 million, 10 points. At $100 million, just 5 points.
None of this proves a second investment would pay off, and none of it proves it wouldn't. What it shows is the combination of assumptions that would have to hold true for the investment to break even.
I couldn't find a Hawaiʻi-specific estimate of how much public characterization actually improves a geothermal resource's odds of reaching commercial development. Past research here tells us something about whether geothermal resources exist at all — that's a different question from how much public spending moves the odds of commercial success. The broader literature also shows substantial uncertainty around geothermal exploration and how "success" should be defined (Wall & Dobson, 2016; Witter et al., 2019).
So rather than manufacturing a number where the evidence doesn't support one, I'm treating Δp and V_public as assumptions I'm testing, not facts I'm asserting.
Who Actually Gets the Value?
There's a second wrinkle: what counts as "public" value in the first place. A successful project would generate revenue for whoever develops it privately, but that revenue doesn't automatically flow back to taxpayers, so I've left it out of V_public.
The public side is broader — things like geothermal royalty revenue, avoided fossil-fuel or ratepayer costs, avoided emissions damage, and energy security. None of these can be collapsed into one clean, Hawaiʻi-specific dollar figure without assumptions about plant size, output, fuel prices, emissions, royalty terms, land tenure, and other details of a project that doesn't exist yet. That's why I tested a range of values for V_public instead of pretending to know a single number.
Distribution matters too. Hawaiʻi already has mechanisms that route geothermal value to specific parties. State law sets aside a county share of geothermal royalties (Haw. Rev. Stat. § 182-7(c)), and geothermal resources on Hawaiian home lands are treated differently. The state Attorney General has determined that royalties from geothermal development on Hawaiian home lands go to the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands under the Hawaiian Homes Commission Act (Department of Hawaiian Home Lands [DHHL], 2014). Hawaiʻi County has also directed geothermal royalty revenue toward relocation and community benefits in Lower Puna (County of Hawaiʻi, 2024). These examples don't tell us how the benefits of a hypothetical Maui project would actually get divided up. Land tenure and project structure would matter enormously here.
A project can create real economic value while the split between developers, taxpayers, host communities, and Native Hawaiian beneficiaries looks completely different depending on how it's structured.
Recommendation
Given all this uncertainty, the sensitivity analysis shows a second $5 million investment could generate positive public value under several combinations of Δp and V_public — the issue is we don't have the confidence to say which combination, if any, actually reflects what would happen on Maui.
The current $5 million is already committed. Rather than doubling down before we've seen those results, I'd treat this program as a natural decision point. Whether to fund a second round should depend on what we actually learn about the resource, how useful the characterization turns out to be, and whether there's a realistic path from better information to real public value.
Waiting does have an opportunity cost. Holding off on another round could delay additional information, development, and the public benefits that might eventually come with geothermal. But committing another $5 million before seeing the results of the current program has an opportunity cost too. The first $5 million is already committed, so the next decision should be treated as a marginal one: does the expected benefit of another $5 million actually exceed its cost? Right now, I don't think we have enough evidence to answer that. Since the current program is already producing information that should reduce some of that uncertainty, I think the value of waiting for that information outweighs the case for making the next investment early.
If the results do support continuing, I'd attach two conditions to future funding.
First, whatever public characterization produces needs to be accessible and reusable. The current program is set up to produce analysis and a published report, but nothing I reviewed says whether the underlying data itself will be broadly available, in what form, or to whom (DBEDT, 2025; HSEO, n.d.). That matters economically — if taxpayers are footing the bill for information that could help future developers, researchers, utilities, and policymakers, the value of that spending can extend well past a single project. If instead it mostly hands one developer a private information edge, the public-good case gets a lot weaker.
Second, future funding should come with transparent reporting on the public value it creates and how that value gets split up. If the case for public investment rests partly on future royalties, lower energy costs, energy security, environmental benefits, or community benefits, then policymakers and taxpayers should be able to check whether those benefits actually show up and who ends up with them. None of this should override existing legal arrangements, including those covering Hawaiian home lands.
Conclusion
My analysis lays out what would have to be true for a second $5 million investment to break even, but it doesn't establish that those conditions currently hold. So my recommendation is to finish and evaluate the current program before committing to another comparable round. If the results support continuing, future funding should make sure the information stays broadly useful and that the public value used to justify the spending can actually be identified and checked.
