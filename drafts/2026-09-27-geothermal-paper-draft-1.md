Should Hawaiʻi Keep Funding Geothermal Resource Characterization?
Hawaiʻi has already put $5 million into characterizing geothermal resources on Maui. That money is paying for slim-hole drilling, testing of the subsurface and water resources, data analysis, updates to the conceptual models, and a set of recommendations for what to do next. The real question isn't whether geothermal energy could someday help Hawaiʻi. It's whether spending public money to reduce our uncertainty about the resource is worth it, and whether the state should follow up with another round of comparable funding.
My recommendation: finish and evaluate the current $5 million program before committing to another one. If the results end up justifying more investment, any future characterization funding should come with two strings attached: the information it produces needs to be accessible and reusable, and there needs to be transparent reporting on who actually benefits from that value and how it's distributed.
What $5 Million Actually Buys
It's worth being precise about what this money is for. It's not building a power plant. It's paying to learn more about what's actually down there. The program funds subsurface and water-resource characterization, testing and logging, data collection and analysis, updated models, and a findings report.
That distinction matters economically. Information itself can be valuable before any commercial project ever breaks ground. Geothermal development takes a lot of upfront exploration money, and a lot of that spending happens while big questions about the resource are still unanswered. Better information lets developers and policymakers make sharper calls with less guesswork, including the call to walk away if the resource doesn't pan out.
There's also a spillover benefit when that information isn't locked up with one company. The Department of Energy's Geothermal Data Repository is a good model for this: it exists specifically to give the public free access to data from DOE-funded geothermal projects, cut down on duplicated effort, and support further discovery. I read that as a public-good argument in action: information gets more valuable once it's not just useful to whoever happened to fund it first. That said, this is my read of what DOE's program structure implies, not a claim that DOE frames it as fixing a market failure.
That distinction matters because I didn't find any evidence that private geothermal developers in Hawaiʻi are systematically underinvesting in characterization. High upfront costs and uncertainty aren't, by themselves, proof that the government needs to step in. So I'm not treating the existence of the current program as automatic justification for a second round. Whatever comes next should be judged on the public value the characterization itself creates.
What Would Have to Be True for a Second $5 Million to Pay Off?
Here's the problem: the two numbers that matter most can't be pinned down with any real confidence right now. One is how much publicly funded characterization actually moves the needle on a project's odds of succeeding commercially — call that Δp. The other is the total public value created if the project does succeed — V_public.
Instead of forcing a single estimate out of numbers that don't support one, I ran a sensitivity analysis. The model is simple:
Expected public value from characterization = Δp × V_public
Net expected public value = (Δp × V_public) − $5 million
Which means the break-even point is:
Δp* = $5 million / V_public
The question this is really asking is: how much would characterization need to improve the odds of success for a second $5 million to break even?

I tested this against assumed public values of $10 million, $25 million, $50 million, and $100 million — not because those are real estimates of what a Maui project would generate, but to see how the answer shifts. And it shifts a lot. At $10 million in public value, characterization would need to improve the odds of success by 50 percentage points to break even. At $25 million, that drops to 20 points. At $50 million, 10 points. At $100 million, just 5 points.
None of this proves a second investment would pay off, and none of it proves it wouldn't. What it shows is the combination of assumptions that would have to hold true for the investment to break even.
That's a real limitation, not a footnote. I couldn't find a Hawaiʻi-specific estimate of how much public characterization actually improves a geothermal resource's odds of reaching commercial development. Past research here tells us something about whether geothermal resources exist at all — that's a different question from how much public spending moves the odds of commercial success. Even the broader literature on geothermal exploration admits there's a lot of uncertainty in this stage, and no clean, agreed-upon "success rate" to lean on.
So rather than manufacturing a number where the evidence doesn't support one, I'm treating Δp as an assumption I'm testing, not a fact I'm asserting. Same goes for V_public.
Who Actually Gets the Value?
There's a second wrinkle: what counts as "public" value in the first place. A successful project would generate revenue for whoever develops it privately, but that revenue doesn't automatically flow back to taxpayers, so I've left it out of V_public.
The public side is broader — things like geothermal royalty revenue, avoided fossil-fuel or ratepayer costs, avoided emissions damage, and energy security. The problem is none of these can be collapsed into one clean, Hawaiʻi-specific dollar figure without stacking on a lot more assumptions about plant size, output, fuel prices, emissions, royalty terms, land tenure, and other details of a project that doesn't exist yet. That's exactly why I tested a range of values for V_public instead of pretending to know a single number.
Distribution matters too. Hawaiʻi already has some mechanisms that route geothermal value to specific parties. State law sets aside a county share of geothermal royalties, and geothermal resources on Hawaiian home lands are treated differently. The state Attorney General has determined that royalties from geothermal development on Hawaiian home lands go to the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands, under the Hawaiian Homes Commission Act. Hawaiʻi County has also directed geothermal royalty revenue toward relocation and community benefits in Lower Puna. These examples show that public value can be routed through real, identifiable channels — but they don't tell us how the benefits of a hypothetical Maui project would actually get divided up. Land tenure and project structure would matter enormously here.
That's another reason I wouldn't evaluate public spending on characterization just by looking at how big a potential project might be. A project can create real economic value while the split between developers, taxpayers, host communities, and Native Hawaiian beneficiaries looks completely different depending on how it's structured.
Recommendation
Given all this uncertainty, I don't think the evidence points clearly toward "obvious success" or "obvious failure." The sensitivity analysis shows a second $5 million investment could generate positive public value under several combinations of Δp and V_public — the issue is we don't have the confidence to say which combination, if any, actually reflects what would happen on Maui.
That's exactly why the current $5 million program is worth treating as a learning opportunity rather than a sunk decision. The money's already committed, and it's set up to produce real data, analysis, updated models, and recommendations. Rather than doubling down before we've seen those results, I'd treat this program as a natural decision point. Whether to fund a second round should depend on what we actually learn about the resource, how useful the characterization turns out to be, and whether there's a realistic path from better information to real public value.
If the results do support continuing, I'd attach two conditions to future funding.
First, whatever public characterization produces needs to be accessible and reusable. The current program is set up to produce analysis and a published report, but nothing I reviewed says whether the underlying data itself will be broadly available, in what form, or to whom. That matters for accountability, but it also matters economically — if taxpayers are footing the bill for information that could help future developers, researchers, utilities, and policymakers, the value of that spending can extend well past a single project. If instead it mostly hands one developer a private information edge, the public-good case gets a lot weaker.
Second, future funding should come with transparent reporting on the public value it creates and how that value gets split up. If the case for public investment rests partly on future royalties, lower energy costs, energy security, environmental benefits, or community benefits, then policymakers and taxpayers should be able to check whether those benefits actually show up, and who ends up with them. None of this should override existing legal arrangements, including those covering Hawaiian home lands — transparency is meant to sit alongside those obligations, not replace them.
None of this requires Hawaiʻi to know today exactly what a future project would look like. It just asks for something more practical: if public money keeps absorbing some of the risk in geothermal exploration, the information and value that spending produces should be traceable back to it.
Conclusion
Geothermal characterization may eventually justify more public investment, but the evidence we have right now doesn't support treating that as a foregone conclusion. The $5 million already spent gives Hawaiʻi a real chance to learn more about its geothermal resources, while also generating evidence about whether publicly funded characterization is worth the cost in the first place.
My analysis lays out what would have to be true for a second $5 million investment to break even. It doesn't establish that those conditions currently hold. So my recommendation is to finish and evaluate the current program before committing to another comparable round. And if the results do support continuing, future funding should make sure the information stays broadly useful, and that the public value used to justify the spending can actually be identified and checked.
