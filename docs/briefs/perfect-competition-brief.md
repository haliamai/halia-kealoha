# Perfect Competition — Engagement Brief

## The Problem
The farm needs to decide how many of its 64 beds to use for tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun to maximize profit. The maximum planting limits add up to 70 beds, so all three crops cannot reach their maximum. The farm also has to consider labor costs, which are added in $25,000 increments, and whether a crop reaches its planting limit or its marginal cost eventually becomes greater than its price because of diminishing returns.

## What I Am Assuming
I’m assuming we should try to use all 64 beds since leaving beds empty feels like a missed opportunity. However, I’m also assuming the model may show that using all 64 beds isn’t actually the most profitable option once labor costs are considered.

## Hypothesis
I predict 20 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 24 mesclun beds. I know the labor required for tomatoes increases much faster at 10% per additional bed compared with 2.5% for carrots and 1.25% for mesclun, but I think the higher return on tomatoes will still make it worth planting all 20 beds. I expect carrots to also reach 20 beds, with the remaining 24 going to mesclun because it requires less labor as production increases.

## How I Would Know I Was Wrong
I would know I was wrong if the model shows that the most profitable mix uses fewer than 17 tomato beds. I would consider 17–20 tomato beds close enough to support my prediction, but anything below that would mean I underestimated how much the 10% increase in labor adds up as more tomato beds are planted.
